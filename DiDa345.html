The Crash You Didn't See Coming

One day you wake up and you get a notification about a crash from your citizen app.Somewhere in Brooklyn last night, there was another crash. Another injury logged into a city database. Another number added to the tally. You probably didn't hear about it as most of these crashes never make the news. Nobody famous got hurt, a random pedestrian just got hurt, filed a police report, and just went home or didn't. That's how it works in Brooklyn where more than 17 crashes happen every single day. I grew up in a Chinese immigrant household. My family and I have worked in the restaurant industry since I was born. I know what it looks like when someone comes home after a 14-hour shift and has to drive back through Sunset Park or Flushing at midnight taking the Belt Parkway, the biggest highway that connevts Brooklyn to Queens because that is the only way home. The people in this dataset are not just a statistics to me. They are people who live in my neighborhoods, eat at the same restaurants, and share the same streets.

Every day in Brooklyn, thousands of drivers make small decisions that can affect your life, like checking a phone, misjudging a gap, cutting through traffic, not looking before backing out, or unnecessary speeding. During the first quarter of the new year, those decisions led to 1,196 crashes, 604 injuries, and one death, all in Brooklyn. The most obvious suspect is distracted driving, as it is the cause of one in every 3 crashes. We see it on the news, in headlines, in campaigns, and even in policy around road safety. If you ask someone on the street what the main cause of crashes is, chances are they are going to say distracted driving.

But the behavior that causes the most crashes is not the same as the one sending people to the hospital. Drivers who fail to yield, speed, and make improper turns each have injury rates over 50%. Even though distracted driving causes 1 in 3 crashes, the chances of it injuring someone are less than 50%. The most common problem is not always the most harmful. This article will answer a simple question: What kind of drivers in Brooklyn are actually putting people at risk?

The most common problem is not always the most harmful

This report analyzes 1,196 motor vehicle collisions reported in Brooklyn between January 1, 2026, and March 9, 2026. The data comes from the NYC Open Data Motor Vehicle Collisions database. Brooklyn is home to over 2.6 million people and records crashes from all hours of the day, all days of the week, across a range of contributing factors.

The Usual Suspect

The data shows one clear trend: driver inattention caused 394 of the 1,196 crashes, one in every three. That is more than four times the count of the next highest cause. The data also shows that every single crash in this dataset involved a sedan. But according to Transportation Alternatives, SUV ownership in Brooklyn rose 25% between 2016 and 2020 while sedan ownership fell 17%. The crashes in this report reflect the entirety of New York not just a borough like Brooklyn. The rise of SUVs is growing in Brooklyn as well so who knows what tommorrows injuries will look like.

The chart below shows the top contributing factors ranked by crash count. Driver inattention stands alone at the top.It is easy to see why this is where the conversation always goes. The number is just so much bigger than everything else.

But here is what that chart does not show you. Of those 394 distracted driving crashes, 188 people were injured. That is a 47.7% injury rate. More than half of all distracted driving crashes hurt nobody.The number is big, but a lot of it is just noise.

"Distraction causes 1 in 3 crashes, but injures someone less than 50% of the time."

Most Common Does Not Mean Most Harmful

Injury rate tells us something crash count cannot. It measures the share of crashes in each category that actually hurt someone. And when you look at it this way, the picture changes completely.The chart below ranks each contributing factor by how often it produces an injury. This is the chart that should be driving policy.

Look at where distracted driving sits on that chart. It is not at the top. It is not even close to the top. Failure to Yield Right-of-Way has a 76% injury rate, that means out of 100 crashes caused by this behavior, 76 hurt at least one person. That is nearly double the rate for distracted driving. When a driver fails to yield, the person they hit is already moving. The collision happens at combined speed. Pedestrians get the worst of it because they have nothing to protect themselves from the impact.

Traffic Control Disregarded, which means running a red light or stop sign, shows an almost 144% injury rate. It produced 46 injuries across just 36 documented crashes.That is more than one person hurt per crash on average.When two cars meet at full speed in an intersection because one driver ignored a light, the damage does not stop at just one person, you could be risking a whole family. Fell Asleep appears only 10 times in the dataset but produced a 50% injury rate. A driver who falls asleep does not brake. The car hits whatever is in front of it at full speed. Compare that to Backing Unsafely is the second most common cause with 85 crashes but only a 20% injury rate. These are 5mph parking lot incidents. They damage bumpers more than people. They are common and almost never serious. Crash count made Backing Unsafely look dangerous but when you look at the Injury rate it tells you the truth.

"The behavior we talk about most is not always the behavior doing the most harm."

When Crashes Happen

6pm

84 crashes in just a single hour

It is not random, Crashes cluster at a specific time of day, and that time is the evening commute. The chart below shows every hour of the day. You seethe spike on the right side of the chart. That is 6pm, with 84 crashes in a single hour. The 3pm to 6pm window alone produced 303 crashes, that is more than 25% of everything in this dataset, in just three hours.

Think about what 6pm looks like on a Brooklyn street. The subway is packed, drivers are cutting across lanes to get home just a bit faster, delivery drivers are double-parked, students are walking home from school or hanging out with friends, The light is fading, And every driver on that road has been awake for at least ten hours due to the long workday. That combination does not produce careful driving.

The midnight hour also shows 70 crashes, the third highest of any hour. This is partly due to the reality of a borough where restaurants and bars stay open late and delivery drivers never stop.

Where Brooklyn Crashes

Brooklyn is not one place. It is seventy neighborhoods packed into 71 square miles, and crashes do not spread evenly across all of them. They concentrate in zip codes with something in common: wide, commerical strips that were built for cars, not for the people who live alongside them.

East New York leads with 63 crashes. Linden Boulevard runs through it. So does Atlantic Avenue and Flatlands Avenue. These are roads designed for moving cars through a neighborhood quickly, not for the people who cross them on foot every day. Canarsie, Bushwick, Crown Heights, Brownsville all of these neighborhoods near the top of this list shares that same design. Wide lanes encourage speeding and speeding turns crashes into injuries.

These are also some of the lowest-income neighborhoods in Brooklyn. They have received less investment in protected crossings, pedestrian signals, and traffic calming than wealthier parts of the borough. Research on pedestrian fatalities shows this is not a coincidence. The streets that kill the most people are almost always in the places with the least power to change them.

Follow the Data, Not the Headlines

Between January 1 and March 9, 2026, one person died on Brooklyn's streets. 604 more were hurt. That is more than 17 crashes a day, every single day. Distracted driving caused the most crashes and it deserves attention. But failure to yield, running traffic controls, and falling asleep at the wheel each send someone to the hospital at a far higher rate. Vision Zero, NYC's road safety program, aims to end all traffic deaths.

Stop building policy entirely around distracted driving. It is common, yes, But the crashes that are actually destroying people are the ones caused by drivers who blow through red lights, fail to yield to pedestrians already in the crosswalk, and speed down the streets of East New York at 6pm on a Tuesday. Those are the behaviors with injury rates above 80% and 90%. Those are the behaviors this data says to target. Put more intersection cameras where failure to yield and traffic control violations cluster. Enforce speed limits on the arterials in the zip codes at the top of that table. Add protected crossings in Canarsie and Brownsville. Make the 3pm to 6pm window the focus of traffic enforcement, not 10am on a quiet residential block. The most common problem gets all the attention. The most harmful problem gets all the injuries. Brooklyn deserves a road safety strategy that knows the difference.
